# A Traveller's Journal: Leilon

[Leilon entry](README.md)

*A work of fiction written for the wiki, in the manner of a chatty, boastful
travel-guide writer. The writer, Ottoline Brackwater, and her publisher's
reader, Hesk Tolliver, are invented for this page. Every place, person, price
and event of Leilon named below comes from the [Leilon entry](README.md)
(circa 1491 DR resettlement snapshot). The conversations, opinions and small
incidents are invented, as are the small sums in the expenses ledger, other than the room,
meal and barge-hire prices. Hesk's marginal notes are in italics.*

## From the Journal of Ottoline Brackwater, Chronicler of Roads, Inns and Other Honest Places

### Ches, 1491 DR. The High Road, a day's walk north of the Mere of Dead Men

My feet hurt, which I record here only so that the historians of the future
will understand the price of scholarship.

I have been told that Leilon is a *ruin*. I have been told this by three
separate carters, a priest of Waukeen who wished to sell me a pamphlet about it,
and a goat. (The goat was less specific.) Yet the road is busy, the sky above
the coast is full of woodsmoke, and I smell, unless I am much mistaken, frying
fish. Ruins do not fry fish. I shall investigate, and you may take it as the
purest coincidence that the investigation will take place near an inn.

*Hesk: The goat was a goat, Ottoline. Please do not put the goat in the book.*

### Later, same day. The gate.

Arrived at dusk by the north-west gate, where a soldier in Neverwinter colours
asked my business. I told him I was Ottoline Brackwater, author of the finest
road guides on the coast, and he asked whether I was carrying any weapons. I
said only my wit. He said that was fine, then; they only worry about the other
kind. I have written this down because it is the nicest thing a guard has ever
said to me.

The gate watch are Neverwinter soldiers posted to the town and the High Road
patrol. I got names out of five of them, **Erlum, Gori, Koz, Emmalou** and a
cheerful soul called **Vester Jessup**, mostly by standing still until they
gave in. Sergeant Yorrum, who commands them, I shall come to.

The town sits on a rise above a long grey mudflat, ringed by an earthen bank
and a wooden palisade, with the Sea of Swords hissing at its western edge.
Dominating everything is a tower, **newly rebuilt**, standing at the centre of
the town like a candle on a cake. I was told that the old High Tower of
Thalivar fell into ruin after the Spellplague, that its wards broke, that the
things it had been guarding wandered out into the streets and that the people
fled. I was told, further, that Lord Protector Dagult Neverember of
Neverwinter ordered the old tower pulled down, and that the town was then
resettled around it. Curious. A lord who is fond of towers is a lord worth
watching, though I have no doubt my readers will want to know what the tower
is *for*. I do not know. Do not ask me.

*Hesk: Thalivar was a wizard who studied the planes and then simply was not
there any more. The wards he left were reportedly fine. Fine is not the same
as safe. Leave the tower alone.*

### The Umber Hulk's Shell Inn

I took a room at the **Umber Hulk's Shell**, the first inn of the resettlement,
kept by a gentleman named **Alion Malwyn**. A room is five silver a night and a
meal one silver, which I consider remarkable, because in Waterdeep I would pay
that for the privilege of being looked at disapprovingly by a doorman.

The inn is outside the wall on the southern shore, near the barge yard. The
beds are clean, the stew is hot, and the name, he tells me, is a long story
that he will tell me for the price of a second drink. I intend to find out
what it is, but I suspect the story is better than the truth, which is all
anyone ever wants from an inn story.

*Hesk: I have it on good authority that there is no umber hulk. There is also
no point telling you this.*

### Day two. Quayside and the Fishery

Up before dawn, which I mention only so that nobody doubts my dedication.
I walked the **boardwalk along the sea wall**, past boathouses and long piers
that reach out over the mud like grey fingers, to the **Fishery** at the end of
Quayside. It is a fish market, a repair shop for nets and tackle, and (in the
evening) the town hall. I have attended many town meetings in many towns and
this is the first at which I have been able to eat my argument.

There I met **Valdi Estapaar**, a half-elf fisher and one of the town's
councillors. It was Valdi, I am told, who got the fishing going again after the
resettlement, and the way he talks about it you would think he had
single-handedly invented the sea. He introduced me to a halfling woman,
**Silla Scalesweep**, who says the herring this season are the finest in
living memory. She also says that about every season. I shall write "fine" and
leave the matter there.

The harbourmaster, **Demelza Tackwind**, would not let me hire a boat until I
had said where I was going and when I would be back. I said "nowhere" and
"soon" and she gave me a look which I have since seen on the faces of
three of my publishers.

*Hesk: A sensible woman.*

### The barge yard

I ought to say something about the **barge yard**, run by a dwarf named
**Rorsta Anvilhand**, because I know my readers admire commerce. Barges are
stored and hired here at **ten gold pieces a barge for ten days**. The sea
off Leilon is not kind: the mudflats are shallow, the tides are cruel, and in
the old days (before the Spellplague) they poled their barges out to ships
anchored offshore and swung the cargo over with cranes. Cranes that stop
working when the wind is high. Which is, I am told, most of the time.

Most trade, then, comes by caravan along the High Road. This is a nice way of
saying that the sea is a *theory* and the road is a *fact*.

### The mines, the fields and the herd

The money in the old Leilon was under the mountains. The eastern mines gave
**copper, nickel and silver**, sold to merchants in Waterdeep, and were guarded
so heavily by the **Lances of Leilon**, some two hundred mounted soldiers,
that they were better defended than most of my publishers' cash boxes. Tunnels
run through the mountains here, and I am told some of them open into the town.
I asked whether the mines are being worked again. Nobody could tell me.
Everyone changed the subject to fish. I record it as unknown, and I record
that I did not volunteer to go and look.

*Hesk: The mines are the gap in the story, as you say. Nothing I have seen
says they have reopened since 1385. Do not tell people they have.*

I was asked more than once whether the town had iron. I could find nobody who
could name an iron mine. I could find a smith, a stall and a great many
people who would like some, which, between us, is how most iron industries
begin.

The land about the town is farmed again, a little at a time. Farms were burned
and flocks driven off in the longship raid of 1358 (it cost the town three
months of ore, which is a very Leilon way to measure a disaster), and the
fields are plainly still finding their feet. Then there are the **goats**, which
I shall come to.

### Settler Camp

Outside the wall, while the rebuilding went on, stood the **Settler Camp**,
run by **Mazira Shae**, where most of the town's business was done before there
were buildings to do it in. The camp has gone quiet now that the houses are up,
but the old hands still say "going to camp" when they mean "going to market".
I like that. I shall steal it.

### The town proper

Within the palisade the new Leilon is built mostly of plain timber, in the way
of a town that wishes to be lived in before it is admired. It is not
the grand stone town of the old books, with its slate roofs and its
**temple of Waukeen** gilded like a fish scale. It is something smaller and
more honest, and I find, to my surprise, that I like it. I am told there
are about two hundred of us. By the number of roofs I counted from
the tower's shadow, there are either a great many more or a great deal of
very large families.

An older Leilon, under a **Lord Pelindar Filmarya**, was many times the size, with
three thousand souls, a rampart, a palisade and a ditch, and a shrine of Tyr
that Pelindar built himself. The old books list a handful of taverns, among
them the Orc's Tusks, the Rutting Buck and the Dusty Gnome, and an inn called
the Sword of Leilon. I looked for them. I found only the new places, which I
suspect is the whole story of Leilon in one sentence.

I visited, in no particular order, because that is the only way I have ever
visited anything:

* **Torver's Post.** The first general store in the resettled town, owned by
  **Jack Torver**, who is also on the council, which makes him the only man I
  have met who can sell you a rope and then vote on whether you may keep it.
  No arms, no armour, no healing potions. Everything else, and a great many
  things you did not know you needed.
* **The Knight's Goblet.** Rebuilt, under the care of a half-elf named
  **Amrisiol Touchfire**, as an inn for officials and the better class of
  traveller, with a rule that no weapons may be carried inside. I find this
  rule *delightful*, as the only blade I own is a letter-opener, and it has
  frequently been confiscated by suspicious clerks.
* **The Peculiarities Shoppe.** Trinkets, curios and a few magic items, kept by
  **Aubrey Silverspun**. Aubrey is dead. Aubrey is also entirely charming and
  not at all inclined to leave. I bought a small brass thing that hums and was
  told that I would know what it was for when the time came. It has hummed ever
  since. I do not know what it is for.
* **The town square.** Much of the buying and selling in Leilon happens here.
  I watched **Zana Taylish**, a blacksmith, sell a sword from a stall to a man
  who then tried to sell it back to her. She declined. He seemed surprised.
  This was, I think, his first day on the road.
* **The stonemason's yard.** **Breltora Red-Eye**, a half-orc, was cutting a
  lintel when I passed. I asked how she liked rebuilding a town that had been
  knocked down once already. She said stone does not care. I wrote that down
  as well.

*Hesk: Aubrey Silverspun was one of the Swords of Leilon, an adventuring
company based in the town. Aubrey died in 1385 with two companions, **Augusta
Krul** and **Ogri Ninebeards**, and all three stayed on as ghosts guarding the
ruins. If you insist on buying things from the dead, do at least pay them.*

### The three shrines

A town that has been through a Spellplague and a resettlement, and a
catastrophe or two since, is a town that has made peace with its gods, and
Leilon keeps three shrines:

* **Lathander**, kept by **Merrygold Brightshine**, a halfling priest who greets
  every traveller as though the dawn had been arranged in their honour.
* **Tymora**, tended by an elf named **Puck Caribdas**, who sells potions and
  luck in roughly equal measure, though not at the same price.
* **Tyr**, under **Vangol Kuskolt**, a dwarf who also sits as the town's judge.
  I asked him whether it was awkward to be both priest and magistrate and he
  said, quite gravely, that the law was a tool of Tyr and the tool was
  working as intended. I paid my fine for jaywalking, which I did not know was
  a crime, immediately.

*Hesk: I would not make a habit of the last.*

### The council and the matter of the tower

The town is run, I gather, by a **council** answerable to Lord Neverember, and
a more diverse assembly I have seldom seen: **Grizzelda Copperwraught**, a
dwarf who is the architect and master of the rebuilding; **Sergeant Hazz
Yorrum** of the Neverwinter garrison; Valdi and Jack Torver, whom you have
already met; and Merrygold and Vangol, of whom the same may be said. They
disagree about almost everything, which is the best sign I know of a council
that works.

Grizzelda, over a mug of something brown, told me that the tower was the
hardest part. "Everything else in this town," she said, "I built. The tower,
I *persuaded*." I wrote that down at once and will be using it at every dinner
party for the rest of my life.

I was also introduced to a Rashemi mage named **Gallio Elibro**, who studies
things and will not say which. I asked what he was researching. He said
"the tower." I asked what about it. He said "yes."

### Concerning the battle

I will not say a great deal about the **Battle of Leilon**, as it was before my
arrival and I make a point of never describing a battle I have not run away from
in person. But I gather the followers of **Talos** and the followers of
**Myrkul** came to blows over the tower, and the town was caught between them
like a pickle at a feast of giants. Fishers' children were driven out of their
homes; soldiers and townsfolk held the line. Two of those children, **Burnice**
and **Smithwell**, children of fishers, still have a way of going quiet when a
bell rings, and I did not ask them why. The tower is, as I say, still
standing. Whether it will be standing when you read this, dear reader, I
cannot promise. I never could promise a thing like that, and I advise against
trusting a guidebook that does.

*Hesk: This is the first sensible thing you have written in this journal.*

### Things I heard and did not verify

* That a black dragon named **Voaraghamanthar** sleeps beneath the Mere of Dead
  Men, and that a cell of the Cult of the Dragon is meant to be in town. I
  have seen no cultists. I have seen a great many people who looked like they
  might be cultists, but it was raining, and everyone looks like that in the
  rain.
* That gnomes from **Ieirithymbul**, in the western Sword Mountains, once came
  to trade every month except in winter, under the watch of a gold dragon
  named **Palarandusk**.
  I am told this still happens. I would very much like to be told this by a
  gnome.
* That the old dwarven halls at **Southkrypt**, east in the mountains, once
  yielded silver, and that stones from them were built into the town.
  Southkrypt is not on my itinerary. Nothing in the mountains is on my
  itinerary.
* That **giant goats** are bred for the town by a gnome named **Pinchwit
  Wigglehoof**, and that part of the herd is sold to a trader in Waterdeep by
  way of Phandalin, through Barthen's Provisions. I met the goat. Its keeper
  was elsewhere, and I had not been told the goat was famous.

*Hesk: So that was the goat. Well, well.*

### A word on the islands

From the pier I could see **Idol Island**, a low green hump in the bay with
what appear to be standing stones, or perhaps statues, along its crown. I
asked three different people what was out there. I received three different
answers, and none was "nothing", which is how I know that something is.

The harbourmaster would not let me go. I said I would pay. She said that wasn't
the problem.

### Day three. The tower, from the outside

I am told that a good guide must walk all the way round a landmark before it
says anything about it, and so, with a bun in one hand and my notebook in the
other, I walked all the way round the tower.

It takes eleven minutes at a stroll and nineteen if you stop to be rude to a
pigeon. The base is old stone, grey and heavy and very much older than the
timber houses around it, and it rises from a low, paved mound, a sort of
raised circle in the middle of the town, as if the rest of Leilon had been
built to give it room to breathe. Above the old courses the tower goes on in
newer stone, a shade paler, laid with the neat, impatient work of someone
who was paid by the day and meant to be paid again tomorrow. The top is a
steep pointed roof, slated, with an iron finial that does not quite catch the
light. There are slits for windows and, so far as I could see, no door on the
side I was looking at. I looked at all the sides. There was a door on
the fourth. It was shut. It had a very polite little sign on it, in the
careful hand of a person who had been asked to put up a sign and had resented
it, which read *PLEASE DO NOT*.

It did not say *do not what*.

I asked the nearest soldier, a lad with a spear who was plainly bored, what
the sign meant. He said it meant what it said. I asked whether a person might
be allowed to knock. He said a person might, and a person might also be allowed
to find out what happened next, and that he had been told to say that to all
the visitors, and that I was the fourth that day, and that it was not yet
lunch.

*Hesk: I have read the account of the Battle of Leilon as it reached
Neverwinter. The town did well to put up a sign. I should add a lock, a ward and a
second sign.*

I am not a woman who disobeys a sign. I am a woman who stands very close to it.
From a few paces off the old stone is warm, and not from the sun, for the
sun was behind a cloud. I laid a hand on it. It felt, for one breath, like
laying a hand on the flank of a sleeping horse, and then it felt like stone
again. I took my hand away and decided that what I had felt was the effect of
the bun, which had been a large one.

I am aware that I have told you very little about the tower, reader. That is
because I have been told very little, and the people who have been told the
most are being careful in a way that I find hard to argue with.

### Day three, afternoon. Grizzelda Copperwraught shows me her building site

I was granted an audience with the architect, which is to say I stood in the
street and she told me to follow her and not touch anything. Councillor
**Grizzelda Copperwraught** is a dwarf of great gravity and little patience,
broad as a barrel and carrying a measuring rod that she uses to point, to
prod and, on one occasion, to move a goat. She wears a leather apron over
a good wool coat, and every one of her pockets is full of something sharp.

She is in charge of the rebuilding, and she knows precisely how much of it
remains to be done, down to the last beam. She told me. It was a long list.
I shall summarise it, as a kindness to my readers, as follows: *a lot*.

"Timber," she said, striking the nearest post with the rod, "is the whole
problem. You can put up a house of timber in a tenday, if you have the timber.
We have the timber for a house in a fortnight and the *need* for a house every
tenday, and so we are always a house behind. Stone is better. Stone lasts. But
stone must be cut, and that wants masons, and that wants time, and my masons
are working on one tower."

"I thought the tower was already built," I said.

She gave me a look. "It was *there*," she said. "That is different."

I asked her how she felt about the palisade and the bank, the great ring
of earth and wood around the town, and she softened, a very little. The wall
is old work, she told me, patched and mended and patched again, and she is
fond of it as one is fond of a difficult relative. "It does what a wall
should," she said. "It stands there and lets everything know that someone cares."

She walked me out to the north-west gate, past the stacks of lumber, the
heaps of shingles and a man who was sawing a log in the wrong direction, to
which she said nothing, but her jaw did something I was glad not to be on the
receiving end of. At the gate she showed me where a stretch of the palisade had
been rebuilt, post by post, and where the older posts stood, grey and tilted.
"We kept those," she said. "Nobody told me to. But when you take down an old
post you take down what it was holding up, and it seemed rude."

*Hesk: A dwarf who respects an old post. I rather like her.*

I must admit I did too. I asked her, as I ask everyone, what was her
favourite thing in Leilon. I expected "the tower." She said, "the fish stew at
the Shell. Don't tell Alion. He'll raise the price."

### Day four. The evening meeting at the Fishery

Tonight, I learned, was the town meeting, held in the evening in the Fishery,
which is also the place where one buys eels. I had been warned that the
meeting would be dull. I had been warned wrong. It was the finest theatre I
have seen in a decade, and I paid nothing for it, which is the best
price for theatre.

The hall is a long, low room above the fish market, smelling of salt, tar and
pickled herring, with benches along the walls and a plank table at one end.
Behind it stood the council: **Valdi Estapaar** in the middle, looking
nervous and proud; **Grizzelda** to his left, arms crossed; **Jack Torver** to
his right with a ledger the size of a paving stone; **Hazz Yorrum** at the end
in the red-and-grey of the Neverwinter garrison, looking as though he would
rather have been outside, in the rain, being shot at; and the two priests,
**Merrygold Brightshine** and **Vangol Kuskolt**, at either hand, the
halfling on a cushion and the dwarf on a stool, so that their heads came
very nearly level.

I will set down the agenda as I recorded it.

*First*, the matter of the fishing boats: whether the Quayside could hold
two more. Valdi was in favour. Jack was in favour *if* the boats paid for the
berths. Grizzelda observed that a berth was a plank, and that she had planks,
and that the matter was therefore concluded. Valdi said she was a marvel.
Jack said she was a marvel with an eye on the lumber budget. A vote was taken
and it was carried by four to two, with the two being Jack and a man in the
back who had not been entitled to vote at all.

*Second*, the matter of the barge yard. **Rorsta Anvilhand** had come in
person, sooty and square, to say that the hire fee of ten gold a barge for
ten days was too low and that he would like it raised. Valdi said the fishers
could not bear it. Rorsta said the fishers could bear a good deal more than
they let on. Jack said that the matter of the fee was a matter of principle,
and a matter of principle was a matter of arithmetic, and a matter of
arithmetic was a matter for a ledger, which he happened to have. The ledger
was produced. The ledger was read. There was a long silence in which one could
hear an eel, somewhere below, rethinking its life.

It was decided that the fee should stay at ten gold for ten days until the
spring catch was counted. Rorsta accepted this the way a man accepts a
cold bath.

*Third*, the matter of Sergeant Yorrum's patrol. **Hazz Yorrum** reported,
in the manner of a man reading from a script he has memorised, that the High
Road had been quiet, that there had been *two* wolf sightings and *one*
cart overturned, and that no one had been killed, which was the whole of
the news and the whole of his ambition. Merrygold, in her small clear
voice, said that she was glad. Vangol asked whether the overturned cart had
been properly inspected for the cause of its overturning. Hazz said that it
had been inspected, and that the cause was the driver. Vangol said that in that
case the matter was one for his court. Hazz said he had hoped it would be.

*Fourth*, the matter of the goats. Here the meeting briefly lost its footing.
A woman in the audience stood up and asked whether the council was aware that
the goats had again been in her turnips. Valdi said the council was aware.
She asked what the council meant to do. Valdi said the council meant to be
aware, firmly. The woman sat down. A voice at the back said that giant goats
in turnips were a matter for **Pinchwit Wigglehoof**, and Merrygold said that
Pinchwit had been sent a note. Grizzelda said she would build a fence. A fence
was added to the list. The list is now longer than the town.

*Fifth*, and last, a matter of the tower. At this point Valdi stood, and
coughed, and said that he had been asked by Lord Neverember's office to say
that all was well, and that the council should take no special action at the
present time, and that the people of Leilon should go about their business,
and that they should *please* go on keeping away from the north side. A
silence followed. Everyone was extremely careful not to look at anyone else.
Then a child, somewhere near the back, asked why. Several adults answered at
once. I did not take down what was said, because it was all different.

*Hesk: You have recorded the whole meeting. You have not recorded what you
thought of it.*

I thought, Hesk, that I should like to live in a town where a fence and a
fortune are discussed in the same breath, and where the whole company,
from the priests to the eels, has an equal right to a grievance. I have not
seen better government. I have seen grander.

The meeting ended with a vote to adjourn, which was unanimous, and with an
invitation, from Valdi, to stay and sample the evening catch, which was also
unanimous, if rather louder.

### Day five. The caravan comes in

A caravan arrived from the south in the middle of the morning, eleven wagons
and about thirty people and a good deal of dust, led by a woman with a face
like a hatchet and a voice like a trumpet whom I will not name, because she
has since asked me not to. They had come up the High Road from Waterdeep,
past the Mere of Dead Men, and by the end of the day the whole of Leilon had
come down to the gate to see what they had brought.

I have always loved a caravan. A caravan is a town on wheels, a town that
brings you all the things you did not know you had been missing until you saw
them for sale. I walked the line of wagons with my hands behind my back, like
a general, and I noted what I saw.

There was cloth, bolts of it, brown and blue and a startling yellow. There
were tools: spades, picks, axes, nails by the keg. There was salt, and flour,
and dried fruit in barrels, and cheese in wax. There were lamp oil and
candles and rope. There were three crates marked *FRAGILE* in a hand that
suggested the sender had had bad experiences. There was, I swear, a barrel
of pickled lemons, which I bought, for I am not a fool.

Nearly everything in Leilon that does not come out of the sea or off the goat
comes in on a wagon, and nearly all of it comes from Waterdeep. I asked
the caravan-mistress whether it was a profitable run. She said it was a
run. I asked whether it was dangerous. She said the road was safer than it had
been, that the Neverwinter patrols had helped, and that the road was not safe,
and that the two statements were both true and I should write them both down.
I did.

**Jack Torver** was at the head of the line with his ledger, buying. I
watched him buy a great deal of rope and a small amount of everything else.
He haggles in the manner of a very patient wall. By sundown he had bought
two wagons' worth of goods at a price that left the caravan-mistress looking
as though she had been pleasantly mugged, and he had sold half of it again
to the town by the next morning, at a price that left the town looking much the
same.

*Hesk: A general store is a clever thing. Ask him how it is he can sell
a rope at a profit to the same town that hired him to buy it.*

I did ask. Jack said, "I have never sold a rope to anyone who did not need it."
I said that I thought this rather gave the game away. He said, "Madam, it is
not a game." I have not yet determined whether that was a joke.

By evening the square was full of stalls, and I spent a happy hour at the
stall of **Zana Taylish**, who sells arms and armour from a trestle, and who,
having heard I had been to Torver's Post, explained, with great politeness and
considerable heat, that Torver's does not stock *anything with an edge*, and
that she thought that a pity, and that she had been *saying so* for some time.
I bought a very small knife, for cheese, and she gave me a look of such
profound professional disappointment that I almost bought a sword.

Torver's Post, I should say for the benefit of travellers, is where one buys
rope, lamps, tents, blankets, bedrolls, pans, boots and every other kind of
adventurer's baggage, but not arms, not armour, and not healing potions. If one
wants those, one goes to Zana, or to a shrine. This arrangement keeps the
peace of the town very nicely. I recommend it to every other town.

### Day six. South along the High Road, to the edge of the Mere

On the sixth day I took a walking stick, a flask, a biscuit and the advice of
three people not to go, and I went to look at the **Mere of Dead Men**.

You do not need to go far. A couple of hours' stroll south from the gate along
the High Road, the ground begins to lose its nerve. The grass goes thin, then
grey, then wet, and the road, a stubborn old line of packed earth and stones,
runs along the edge of a great pale marsh as if it had agreed not to look. The
Mere is wide and flat and very quiet. There is water standing in it that is the
colour of a dishcloth, and there are reeds, and there is mist in the morning
that sits on the ground like a cat, and every so often a bird calls out, and
nothing answers.

I will tell you the thing about the Mere that nobody tells you. It is *pretty*.
At sunrise, with the mist pink and gold and the water as flat as a mirror,
it is one of the loveliest sights I have seen on the whole of the Sword Coast.
It is the loveliness of something that has not noticed you. It is the
loveliness of a very large animal asleep.

I sat on a milestone and ate my biscuit, and a patrol of Neverwinter soldiers
came by, five of them, and one of them was **Vester Jessup**, whom I knew
from the gate. He stopped to say hello and to ask what I was doing, and I
said I was *observing*, and he said that was a good word for it, and that in
the Mere you did best to observe from the road.

"Why's that?" I asked.

"Well," said Vester, in the reasonable voice of a man explaining a house rule,
"people go in. People don't come out. That's the main one."

I asked whether this was a matter of the bogs or of something else. He looked
at the sky, and then at the Mere, and then at his boots, and said he'd heard
there was a dragon.

"A black one," said another soldier. "Voaraghamanthar. Sleeps down there, they
say, and every so often has a stretch."

"Has he been seen?" I asked.

"Not by anyone who's said so afterwards," said Vester.

*Hesk: This is, in fact, the traditional account. It is a very old story and
the people of Leilon tell it well. I would not argue with it from the road,
and I would argue with it even less from the Mere.*

We stood a little while in the quiet. A heron flew up out of the reeds and
went away across the water, low and slow, and for a moment the whole Mere
seemed to hold its breath. Then Vester said he had a patrol to finish, and
I said I should be getting back, and we walked north together, which I
considered the sensible course of action for both of us.

On the way back he told me about the road. The High Road, the long stone-and-
earth road from Neverwinter to Waterdeep, is the whole reason for Leilon. The
town is a rest stop, a place to sleep and to be fed and to pay a toll of coin
and gossip before going on. Without the road there would be no town; without the
town there would be no road worth the name, or at any rate no road you would
care to be caught on after dark. He said the patrols had been doubled after the
Battle of Leilon, and that now one could cover the road from the town to the
Neverwinter walls in about four days with a good cart, or two on a fast horse,
and that this was a great improvement. I wrote it down.

### Day six, evening. The Tymora Shrine and a lesson in odds

I went in the evening to the **Shrine of Tymora**, which stands in the east of the town, a short walk from the square, a small, bright, cheerful building
with a doorway painted in a pattern of coins. Its keeper, **Puck Caribdas**,
is an elf of unguessable age with a sharp, merry face and a way of looking at
you as though he knows exactly what you are going to say and is delighted by
it.

Puck sells potions. I had expected, from the shrine's name, to receive a lecture
on the benefit of faith. Instead I received a very practical lecture on
the benefit of *potions*, which he sets out on a long shelf behind the altar,
in neat rows of small, stoppered bottles, each labelled in tiny, elegant
handwriting. Healing, he told me, is the best seller. "But there's a world of
difference," he said, "between a healing potion and a *good* healing potion.
A good one tastes of mint and honey. A bad one tastes of regret."

I bought one of the bad ones for research and drank it on the doorstep. It
tasted of regret. I recommend it to nobody.

We talked for a long time, he and I, about luck. Puck's view of luck is
cheerful and very exact. "It isn't a gift," he said. "It's a *wager*. Tymora
gives you the dice, and she's glad when you roll them. But you must roll."
He told me that Leilon is the luckiest town he has ever lived in, and that it
is also the unluckiest, and that the difference between the two was whether
you chose to be in it.

"The tower stood for a century with nobody the wiser," he said. "Then the
Spellplague. Then the wards. Then the cults. Then the soldiers. Then us. If
you add it up, it's more bad luck than most towns see in a thousand years."

"And the good luck?" I asked.

"We're *still here*," said Puck.

*Hesk: A fine answer. It is the best answer I have heard to any question
about Leilon.*

### Day seven. The court of Tyr

I attended, in a spirit of pure scholarship, the court of **Vangol Kuskolt**,
priest of Tyr and judge of the town. The shrine of Tyr stands in the south-west of the
 town, a round, domed, sturdy building of grey stone, with a scale
carved in the lintel and nothing else by way of decoration. The courtroom
inside is a single room with a raised bench at one end, a rail in the middle,
and a smell of beeswax and honest labour.

The dwarf sat on the bench, in a black robe, with a gavel in one hand and a
mug of tea at his elbow. He looked as I imagine the mountain looks, if
the mountain were required to give a ruling on a goat.

The day's business, I discovered, was one case. It had been heard twice
already and was returning for a third and, as the clerk said, *final* hearing.
It concerned a barge.

Two men, one a fisher and one a carter, had hired a barge together from the
barge yard, at the usual fee of ten gold for ten days, to carry a cargo of
salted fish and lamp oil out to a ship anchored beyond the mudflats. The barge
was loaded. The barge was poled out. The barge reached the ship. The cargo
was transferred. And then the fisher and the carter had begun to quarrel over
which of them owed what portion of the fee, and the barge had drifted, and the
tide had turned, and the barge had been stranded on the mud for the better part
of a night, to the considerable discomfort of everyone concerned, and
considerable benefit of the herring.

Vangol heard the fisher. Vangol heard the carter. Vangol heard the barge master,
Rorsta, who had come to speak for the barge, and who spoke, in his gruff
way, for some time. Then Vangol put down his tea and was silent for about the
length of a prayer.

"The fee," he said at last, "is ten gold for ten days. The barge was used for
one of those days and was stranded for the night of another. The fee is owed
in full, as the barge was held. It is divided between the two of you equally,
as the hire was joint. Neither of you will argue with the other about the
division, or if you do, you will do it *in the street*, where I cannot hear you.
Rorsta shall be paid by sundown. The fish shall be sold. The oil shall be sold.
The tide shall be consulted before the next voyage."

He struck the gavel. It sounded like a door closing on an argument.

I should add that this was, in its way, a perfect piece of justice. Nobody was
satisfied. Everybody was fairly treated. Both men went out into the street and
began quarrelling again at once, but with an air of ritual rather than of
animosity.

*Hesk: You have just described the practice of law.*

Afterwards I spoke with the priest, in the quiet of the empty courtroom. I
asked him what it was like, being both priest and judge. He told me, very
simply, that in a town as young as Leilon there is no one else to do it.
"Someone has to say what's fair," said Vangol. "I'd rather it were me than
someone who doesn't like being wrong."

I asked him whether he ever got it wrong.

"Often," he said. "That's why there's an appeal."

"To whom?"

He pointed upward, and then at the Neverwinter road, and then at his tea.

### Day eight. The story of the Umber Hulk's Shell

I promised my readers that I would discover the origin of the inn's name, and
I am a woman of my word, and at last, on the eighth evening, over a second
drink, as promised, **Alion Malwyn** told me.

You must picture the scene. The common room of the Umber Hulk's Shell, in the
evening: a long low room with a fire at one end and a bar at the other, and
benches, and tables with names scratched into them. It smells of woodsmoke
and stew. The company is mixed: fishers coming off the quay with their boots
wet, soldiers off duty, two or three carters playing dice, a mage in a corner
reading, and the dwarf **Grizzelda**, eating alone, and quite plainly wishing
to be left that way. Alion stood behind the bar, drying a mug with a cloth
that was no cleaner than the mug, and he leaned forward and lowered his voice,
and the room, in the way of rooms, leaned in as well.

"It was the first night we opened," he said. "The first inn in the new town.
We had a roof, four walls, and a sign. The sign wanted painting. I had a
brush and a pot of paint and a name in my head, and a *very* bad cold.
And the name I had in my head was the *Umber Hulk's Hearth*."

"That is a good name," I said.

"It is a good name," Alion agreed. "But I had a cold, and I said it aloud,
and the painter, who was a very literal young man from Neverwinter, heard it
as *Shell*. And by the time I had blown my nose and looked up, he had painted
the whole thing, in gold, in letters a foot high."

"And you did not correct him?"

"The paint was *gold*," said Alion, with dignity. "Do you know what gold paint
costs?"

The room, which had been listening, burst out laughing, and Alion bowed, and
the mage in the corner looked up from her book and said, quite gravely, that
she had heard it was an umber hulk that had once lived in the cellar. Alion
said there had never been an umber hulk in the cellar. The mage said that was
not what the *cellar* said. Alion said the cellar did not talk. The mage said
that it was a matter of listening.

*Hesk: I note that you now have three different accounts of the inn's name.
Well done.*

Dear reader, I have *four*. The fourth I was given by Grizzelda, who said that
it was named for a shell found on the beach, and that the hulk was a joke
added later by a soldier. The fifth, offered by a fisher, was that it was named
for a very large mussel. I have decided that all five are true. This is the
only honest way to write about a tavern.

I went to bed that night in a clean bed in a clean room with a slightly crooked
shutter, and I slept better than I have slept in a month. In the morning there
was porridge. It was a good porridge, thick and hot, and the milk in it, I
suspect, had been milked from a goat.

### Day nine. Dinner at the Knight's Goblet

On the ninth night I dressed for dinner, which is to say I brushed my coat and
put on my second-best hat, and presented myself at the **Knight's Goblet**.

The Goblet is the grandest house in the town, which is a little like being the
tallest hobbit. It stands on the main street not far from the tower, a solid,
two-storey building of old timbers and new plaster, with a carved sign of a
silver goblet over the door and a doorman in a livery of green and white, who
looked me up and down and said, with great politeness, that I would find the
rule of the house on the wall.

The rule of the house is that no weapons may be carried inside. It was painted,
very fine and large, in a neat black hand on a board beside the door, and
beneath it hung a row of pegs on which guests had hung, with every appearance
of reluctance, an astonishing selection of swords, axes, daggers, clubs, a
crossbow, a long chain with a hook on it that I did not like the look of, and a
very small, very polished knife that I took to belong to a man who did not
look like a man with a very small, very polished knife. I hung my
letter-opener on the end. The doorman received it with grave courtesy.

Inside, the room was warm, bright and hushed, the kind of hush that costs a
silver a head. There were round tables with white cloths and candles in glass
chimneys, and a great stone hearth, and, across the back wall, the
trophies of a past that was not quite the house's own: a banner, a boar's
head, a shield with a design I could not read. The company was of the better
class. There was a Neverwinter clerk in a clean collar, two traders from the
south, a lady in a green cloak who was plainly someone's wife or someone's
agent, a man who wished to be taken for a priest and was not, and a lean grey
officer of the garrison who was dining alone with the grim determination of
a soldier at a banquet.

The house is kept by **Amrisiol Touchfire**, a half-elf in his middle years with
grey at the temples, a straight back and a face that had settled, over the
years, into an expression of unruffled welcome. He came to my table himself,
and bowed, and asked whether I had everything I wished.

"I wish to know how a house like this comes to be in a town like this," I
said.

He smiled. It was a rather lovely smile, I thought, the smile of a man who
has been asked this before and does not mind. "The Goblet is older than
Leilon," he said. "Or rather, the *name* is. There was a Knight's Goblet
before the Spellplague, in the old town, in the days of Lord Pelindar. A
tavern of good repute. They said it served a whole roast boar to any traveller
who asked for it. I should like to tell you that I brought the boar back. I
have not. I serve a very fine game pie."

I asked how he had come by the name.

"I rebuilt the house," he said, "and I looked at the old sign, which was in
the ashes, a little scorched. And I thought that a town that is coming back
ought to have some things that have come back with it. A name is a cheap thing
to save, and a dear thing to lose."

I wrote that down, and then I ate the game pie, which was very fine, with a
sauce of apples and something like juniper, and a cheese that was both
a surprise and a pleasure. The wine was Waterdhavian and had the sort of
price that I will not repeat in print, for fear of being asked to pay it again.

I note, for the practical reader, that the Goblet is the house for officials,
wealthy visitors and anybody who wishes to talk of business without being
overheard by a fisherman. For everybody else there is the Shell.

*Hesk: You will not be surprised to learn that the old Knight's Goblet is
mentioned in a famous guide to the North, by a writer of whom I shall say only
that she had a much smaller notebook and a much larger reputation.*

### Day ten, morning. The goat-herd

I had meant, from the first day, to meet the goat. I do not mean the goat I had
already met, the one on the road, whose acquaintance I regard as complete. I
mean the *goats*, the great shaggy flocks of giant goats that graze the hills
above the town, and their keeper, the gnome **Pinchwit Wigglehoof**, of whom
I had heard so much.

I found him, after an hour's climb, on a hillside north of the town, sitting
on a stone, in a hat the size of a cartwheel, with a staff across his knees
and, around him, thirty-odd goats the size of ponies.

I do not say that these are small goats. I say that I have seen a goat look a
man in the eye, over the top of his *hat*, and I was that man.

Pinchwit is round and brown and cheerful, with a long moustache that curls at
the ends, and he talks, I regret to say, very much as I do, which is to say
without ever coming up for air. He told me the names of all the goats. There
were thirty-one. I remember four, one of which, a huge grey nanny with a
bell around her neck, is called **Madam Chancellor**, and is the dominant
goat, and has been, he assures me, for six years.

"They're good beasts," he said. "They give milk, which makes a very fine
cheese. They give meat, if you've the heart for it, which I haven't, mostly.
And they give *hair*, madam, the finest goat's hair in the North, warm as a
hearth and tough as a harness. A cloak of it'll last a lifetime. I'd
not sell my own cloak for a hundred gold."

I asked about the sale of the flock to the south.

"Ah," said Pinchwit, and his moustache twitched. "That's a delicate matter,
that is. You've heard, I expect, there's a trader in Waterdeep who takes a
part of the flock. I send them by way of Phandalin, you see, to a friend
there, a Mr. Barthen, who keeps a store. He's a good man. He takes them off
my hands and sends them on, and the trader pays, and I get my share. It's the
best arrangement I've ever had with a human."

I asked him how he felt about the turnips.

A long silence.

"What turnips?" said Pinchwit.

*Hesk: A man of wisdom.*

I stayed an hour on that hillside, drinking goat's milk out of a wooden cup,
and by the end of it, I assure you, Pinchwit and I were firm friends. He
showed me how to milk a goat, which I did badly, and how to tell, from the look
of its ear, whether it was in a good mood, which I did worse. I was bitten once,
firmly, by a young billy named **Dumpling**. I record this because it was, in its
way, a compliment.

### Day ten, afternoon. A word with the mage

I had, I confess, been avoiding **Gallio Elibro**.

Not out of fear. Out of *tact*. A man who studies a tower, and who will not say
what he is studying about it, is a man whom one asks, on the whole, only once.
But on the tenth day I had made up my mind to ask a second question, and I
found him, at last, in the late afternoon, sitting on a low wall near the old
mound with a book open on his knee, in a patch of sun, looking as though he had
been there all day.

Gallio is a Rashemi, as I have said, lean and dark and about forty, with a
long, serious face and ink on his sleeves, and a pair of round spectacles that
he pushes up his nose when he is thinking. He speaks Common with a slight
rolling accent, carefully, as though each word had been weighed on a scale.

I sat down beside him, uninvited, and offered him a bun.

He considered it, and took it.

"I will tell you what I can," said Gallio, "which is not very much. I am not
here to hide things, madam. I am here because the tower was built by a man
who wished to study the planes, and left a great deal of notes, and the
notes are all that remain of him. I am reading the notes."

"And what do they say?"

"They say," said Gallio, and pushed up his spectacles, "that the man was
brilliant and very careless. They say that he built wards against many things,
and not against the *one* thing that happened. They say that wards are like
walls. They keep out what you thought of. They do not keep out what you did
not."

He fell silent, and looked at the tower, which in the afternoon light was
very still and very grey.

"Is it safe?" I said.

"Is anything?" said Gallio. "It is *quiet*. For the present, that will do."

*Hesk: A sentence that I have underlined twice.*

He would say no more, and I did not press him. I sat on the wall for a while,
sharing the sun and the silence and the last of the bun, and watched the
shadow of the tower grow long across the town.

It occurred to me that I had spent ten days in Leilon, and that in all that
time I had met the councillors and the shopkeepers and the priests and the
soldiers and the goats, and had been given the whole story of the place in
a hundred different voices, and that not one of them, not *one*, had said that
they wished themselves elsewhere.

### Final entry, Ches 1491 DR. The north-west gate.

I leave Leilon with a sore foot, a humming brass object of no purpose, an
empty purse and a very full journal. I have eaten the fish, climbed the bank,
bought from the dead, been fined by a priest, and not been permitted to land
on an island. By any reasonable standard it has been a triumph.

Will it be a great town again? That, I think, depends on whether the people
in it can keep the council from arguing, the cults from fighting and the
tower from remembering what it was for. On present evidence, I would bet
on the first two. The third is anyone's guess.

Come and see it before it improves. I am Ottoline Brackwater, and I recommend
**the fish.**

*Hesk: She recommends the fish because she did not pay for the fish.*

## Appendix A. A Practical Guide for the Traveller

*Compiled by Ottoline Brackwater on the coach home, from notes made in the field
and revised, against her will, by Hesk Tolliver.*

I have never believed in a guide that does not tell you where to sleep. A guide
that talks only of history is a grave marker. Here, then, is Leilon as a
traveller will find it in the spring of 1491 DR, set down plainly, in the
order that a traveller will need it.

### Getting there

Leilon stands on the **High Road**, the long road between Neverwinter and
Waterdeep, on a shallow mudflat at the edge of the Sea of Swords, with the
Sword Mountains rising behind it to the east and the Mere of Dead Men lying
just to the south. Whichever way you come, you will arrive by road. The
sea is not a way in. It is a way of getting your feet wet.

From the north, the road runs down from Neverwinter, past Helm's Hold, and
enters the town by the north-west gate. From the south it comes up along the
very edge of the Mere, and a traveller who has walked it knows exactly when
the Mere has ended, because the air suddenly smells of woodsmoke and fish
instead of nothing in particular. Both ways are patrolled by soldiers of the
Neverwinter garrison, and the sergeant of the garrison, **Hazz Yorrum**, sits on
the town council, so complaints about the roads go straight to the man who can
do something about them, or at any rate be sorry.

Phandalin lies in the hills to the north-east. Travellers from there come down to the
High Road and follow it in, though I could not find that any track runs direct to
the town gate.

### Where to sleep

| House | Keeper | For whom | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The Umber Hulk's Shell** | Alion Malwyn | Everyone | The first inn of the resettlement. Rooms five silver a night, meals one silver. Outside the wall, on the southern shore, near the barge yard. Clean beds, hot stew, an unreliable story about the name. |
| **The Knight's Goblet** | Amrisiol Touchfire | Officials, wealthy travellers, anybody with business to discuss quietly | No weapons inside; hang yours on a peg by the door. Game pie, Waterdhavian wine, a hearth worth the price. Dear. |
| **The Settler Camp** | Mazira Shae | Nobody, now | A temporary camp outside the wall where most business was done during the rebuilding. Quiet now that the houses are up. |

*Hesk: You will notice the omission of the old taverns and of the Temple of
Waukeen. They are described in the old books, and I find no sign of them in the new town,
and a guide that sends a traveller to a tavern that
has been burned down is not a guide but a joke.*

### Where to eat

The **Shell** serves the fish stew, which the town's architect considers the
best thing in Leilon. The **Goblet** serves game pie, and, I am told, on a
good day, something that nobody has yet been able to describe to me without
sighing. The **Fishery** on Quayside sells fish, fresh and pickled and smoked,
and in the evening, if you have timed it right, you may eat your supper at
the town meeting. For breakfast, find a goat.

### What to buy, and where

- **Rope, tents, bedrolls, lamps, boots, pans, rations, all the baggage of
  the road:** **Torver's Post**, run by Jack Torver. *Not* arms, not armour, not
  healing potions.
- **Arms and armour:** **Zana Taylish's stall** in the town square. She will
  tell you at length why Torver's does not stock them.
- **Potions:** the **Shrine of Tymora**, kept by Puck Caribdas. Healing potions
  are the best seller. Choose the good ones.
- **Trinkets, curios, and a very few magic items:** **Aubrey's Peculiarities
  Shoppe**, kept by a ghost. Ask what the item does. Do not expect a clear
  answer.
- **Fish, and fishing gear, and the repair of fishing gear:** the **Fishery**.
- **Barges, to hire:** the **barge yard**, run by Rorsta Anvilhand. Ten gold
  pieces a barge for ten days, at the time of writing. The tide is not
  included.
- **Cheese, milk and goat's hair cloth:** the goat-herd on the north hill, if you
  can find him and he likes you.

### What the shrines are for

Leilon keeps three shrines, each in its own corner of the town.

- **The Shrine of Lathander**, under Merrygold Brightshine, for healing, for
  the dawn, and for the kind of encouragement that a halfling of great good
  cheer can supply at six in the morning.
- **The Shrine of Tymora**, under Puck Caribdas, for luck, and for potions.
- **The Shrine of Tyr**, under Vangol Kuskolt, for judgement, and for the hearing
  of disputes. If you have a quarrel, you may take it to Vangol. Be warned that
  he will decide it.

### What to avoid

- **The north side of the tower.** There is a sign. The sign says *PLEASE DO
  NOT*. Do not.
- **The Mere of Dead Men, beyond the road.** People go in. People do not come
  out. There is a story about a black dragon, and nobody I met was keen to put
  it to the test.
- **Idol Island.** The harbourmaster will not let you land, and has not said
  why. This is the best reason I know for believing there is a reason.
- **Walking the mudflats at low tide.** The mud is deeper than it looks and
  faster than you are.
- **Asking the council about the goats and the turnips in the same sentence.**

### When to come

The High Road is busiest in the warm months, and the caravans run most
often then. The gnomes of Ieirithymbul, in the western Sword Mountains, are
said to come down to trade once a month, except in winter, so that a
traveller who wishes to see them should come between spring and autumn. The
sea is at its kindest in summer. The Mere is at its loveliest at sunrise, in any
season, from a safe distance.

## Appendix B. How Leilon Earns Its Bread

*An account of the town's trade, set down for the benefit of any reader with a
sense of arithmetic. Hesk has gone over it, line by line, and has struck out
three of my adjectives.*

A town is a machine for turning one set of things into another set of things,
and I will describe the machine as I found it.

**What goes in.** Nearly everything that is not fish, goat, timber or the
work of someone's hands comes into Leilon by **caravan along the High Road**,
most of it from **Waterdeep**: cloth, tools, nails, salt, flour, dried fruit,
oil, candles, rope, wine. It is bought, wholesale, by the general store and
by the inns, and sold on to the town. A little comes by sea, by **barge**, from
ships that anchor offshore, but the sea off Leilon is shallow, the tides
cruel, and the wind, for much of the year, an enemy of any crane, and the town
takes its trade by the road and its water by the bucket.

**What comes out of the sea.** The **fishery** is the youngest industry in
the new town and the one of which the town is proudest, revived after the
resettlement by Valdi Estapaar and the fishers who followed him. The catch is
sold at the Fishery on Quayside, fresh, pickled and smoked, and what is not
eaten in Leilon is carried by cart up and down the High Road. The fleet is
small. The boats are many. The stories are larger than either.

**What comes off the hills.** The **giant goats** of Pinchwit Wigglehoof give
milk, meat and the fine, strong goat's hair. Part of the flock is sold
to a trader in Waterdeep by way of Phandalin and **Barthen's Provisions**. Pinchwit
is not a rich man, but he is the only man in the North who has ever been
asked to explain a goat to a lord.

**What comes out of the ground.** This is the question on which the future of
the town depends. In the old days, before the Spellplague, the **eastern mines**
produced **copper, nickel and silver**, sold to merchants in Waterdeep, and the
town was rich enough to be guarded by the **Lances of Leilon**, two hundred
mounted soldiers. The mines are *there*. The tunnels, as I have said, run
through the mountains, and some of them open into the town. Whether they are
being worked today, I could not discover, and nobody would say. There are
people in Leilon who are working very hard at not saying.

**What comes off the anvil.** There is a **smith** in the town, and a good one.
**Zana Taylish** sells her work from a stall in the town square, and the
Neverwinter garrison, I gather, is among her better customers. There is no
sign of an iron mine, but there is a great deal of demand for iron, and a
town that has demand will, in time, find supply.

**What comes off the farms.** The farms about the town are young and few. They
were burned in the raid of 1358 and have been coming back, slowly, ever
since. Nobody expects them to feed the town. They are, for now, a sign that
people mean to stay.

**What goes out.** Fish; goat's hair, cheese and a few live goats; and, in
the fullness of time, if the mines have reopened, copper, nickel and silver.
Everything else is small.

**The balance.** The town, I should say, runs at a loss, as a young town
should. The money comes in, not from trade, but from **Neverwinter**: the
garrison's pay, the council's budget, the cost of rebuilding. It is a town
carried on a lord's shoulder. Whether that shoulder will grow tired is a
question for a better arithmetician than I.

*Hesk: That is, in outline, the position. Please note, for the record, that the
financial state of Leilon is not stated in any source I have read. You have
invented it from what you saw. It is a sensible invention. It is still an
invention.*

## Appendix C. A Who's Who of Leilon, 1491 DR

## Appendix E. A Letter from the Publisher's Reader

*Hesk Tolliver to Ottoline Brackwater, on the receipt of the manuscript.*

Dear Ottoline,

I have read your journal, all of it, twice, with a pencil, and I have to tell
you that it is the longest and most disorderly account of a small town on the
Sword Coast that has ever crossed my desk, and that I have not enjoyed
anything so much in years.

I have corrected what I could. I have struck out the claims that you could
not support, and the places where you plainly meant to say a thing
and said another, and I have left the jokes where they stand, because a
journal without jokes is a ledger. Where the account of Leilon rests upon
what the sources say, I have checked it. Where it rests upon what you saw
and what you were told, I have said so in the margin. You will find that I
have been fairer to the goat than you deserved.

I should like, in all honesty, to add three remarks, as a reader.

*First*, I have never seen a town so busy being rebuilt, and so little troubled
by being rebuilt. That is not the work of the walls or the tower. It is the
work of the people, who seem to have decided, in a body, that the matter of
Leilon was not up for debate.

*Second*, there are a good many things you do not know, and you have been honest
about most of them. Whether the mines are open. What the tower is for. What is
on Idol Island. Whether the black dragon in the Mere is anything more than a
story told to keep children out of the reeds. I should not wish a reader to
close this journal believing that you had settled any of these questions. You
have not. You have shown that a town can live very well beside them.

*Third*, and I say this as an editor, you have a way of finishing every entry
with the thing you were really afraid of. I counted. It happens on eleven
pages. I would not change it for the world.

Come to Waterdeep when you can. I will buy the fish.

Yours, with the pencil lowered,

**Hesk Tolliver**
